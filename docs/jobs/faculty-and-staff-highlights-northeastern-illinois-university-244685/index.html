Dr. Jeanine NtihiragezaProfessor and Coordinator of SAELL and TESOL What do you do at NEIU? I am a Professor, TESOL Program Coordinator, and founding Director of the Center for Genocide and Human Rights Research in Africa and the Diaspora (GHRAD) at Northeastern Illinois University. As a survivor of the 1972 genocide in Burundi, my work bridges academic scholarship with deeply personal advocacy for human rights and

genocide prevention. What do you research? My research spans multiple interconnected fields: linguistics, language teaching, genocide studies, and human rights in Africa. Since 2013, I have served as Chair of the Genocide and Human Rights Research Group at NEIU, leading an interdisciplinary team that organizes annual conferences bringing together scholars, survivors, and advocates from around the world. For example,

in June 2025, two GHRAD members and I represented GHRAD at the UN headquarters to advocate for the recognition of the genocide that took place in 1972 in Burundi. My scholarly contributions include numerous refereed journal articles and book chapters on linguistics and, more recently, on the African concept of ubuntu in mass atrocity contexts. I am particularly proud of my co-edited and co-authored book, Critical

Perspectives on African Genocide: Memory, Silence, and Anti-Black Political Violence (Rowan and Littlefield International, 2021), as well as my personal chapter "Repenser pour mieux panser: A survivor's account of the 1972 Burundi genocide," which weaves together my academic expertise with lived experience. Drawing on my PhD in Linguistics from the University of Chicago and my fluency in Kirundi, English, and French,

I have been leading the collection of genocide survivor and witness testimonies of the 1972 Burundi genocide for our Digital Oral History Archive. This collaborative effort with GHRAD colleagues and students represents a crucial preservation of voices that might otherwise be lost to history. Since 2022, I have led research trips to Burundi, working directly with communities to document these testimonies and advocate

for recognition of this often-overlooked genocide. My approach centers on Ubuntu—the African philosophy of interconnectedness—as both a framework for understanding mass atrocities and a pathway toward healing and conflict resolution.
